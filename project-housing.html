ECON 349 / COURSE PROJECT / 2026

What makes a place
worth the rent?

Using student housing choices to explore the tradeoffs between price, convenience, and amenities.

StataConditional logitChoice modeling
50survey respondents
500choice tasks
2conditional-logit models

01 / SUMMARY

Every amenity comes with a tradeoff.

In an economics course project, I analyzed supplied housing-choice data for a hypothetical property scenario. The question was how rent and amenities relate to student preferences—and how those patterns could inform a set of housing options.

My role: reconstructing the choice data, merging housing attributes, estimating the models, interpreting willingness-to-pay estimates, and proposing pricing tiers.

02 / METHODS

Model the choice, not just the property.

I organized the data into long format: 50 respondents, ten choice tasks each, and two alternatives per task. Each alternative included attributes such as rent, walking time, room size, furnishing, laundry, and utilities.

The baseline conditional-logit model used ten housing attributes. A second specification added seven interactions with budget and living situation. I then examined coefficient ratios as willingness-to-pay estimates and considered budget, convenience, and premium tiers.

03 / RESULTS

Useful structure, uncertain dollar amounts.

The models provided a framework for comparing amenity bundles and proposing differentiated housing tiers. The sample was small, however, and rent estimates were imprecise; most individual effects were not statistically clear. The results did not support confident dollar-valued premiums.

The resulting recommendations were proposals for the course scenario. There was no real client implementation or measured revenue effect.

04 / TAKEAWAYS

A precise-looking number can still be uncertain.

Willingness-to-pay calculations depend heavily on the estimated rent coefficient. When that estimate is weak, a neat dollar figure can imply more confidence than the data support. Here, the more useful outcome was a way to structure tradeoffs and identify what a larger follow-up survey should test.
