01 / SUMMARY

Finding a question our team could work with.

After hearing about DataFest from my professors, I joined a group of friends to take on the challenge. It was a different team from the datathon, and this time we had 48 hours to analyze healthcare data and present our findings. My focus was care fragmentation: how the number of departments a patient visits relates to emergency-department use.

My role: analyzing unique departments visited and emergency visits among patients with type 2 diabetes, contributing findings, and presenting care-coordination recommendations with the team.

02 / METHODS

Comparing department visits, emergency visits, and overall use.

The analysis compared counts of unique departments visited with emergency visits and encounter volume for patients with type 2 diabetes. This provided a way to examine whether care spread across more departments accompanied greater emergency use.

We combined the care-fragmentation work with other team analyses and organized the findings into a shared presentation and recommendations.

03 / RESULTS

An association to investigate, and a People’s Choice award.

Our analysis found an association between more departments visited and greater emergency-department use. That supported further investigation into coordination across departments, rather than treating every encounter as an isolated event.

Our five-person team earned People’s Choice among more than 20 teams at Northwestern’s competition.

Illness severity and total encounter volume can influence both measures. The analysis did not establish that fragmentation caused emergency visits or demonstrate cost savings.

04 / WHAT I LEARNED

A smaller problem was easier to get started on.

Like the datathon, DataFest was a chance to use what I’d learned in class on a problem that felt intimidating at first. Talking it through with friends and breaking it into manageable questions helped us get moving. It gave me a better perspective on the choices real data scientists have to make when the data don’t come with a ready-made question.

For my part of the analysis, counting departments gave me a starting point, but it couldn’t tell me whether a patient needed specialist care or was experiencing avoidable fragmentation. Before recommending a fix with confidence, I’d want to account more carefully for patient complexity and overall healthcare use.

A different kind of choice.
